The Challenge

Medicare marketing is complex. You're managing multiple product lines (Medicare Advantage, Supplement, Part D), strict CMS compliance rules, tight AEP/OEP enrollment windows, and a demographic that spans tech-savvy boomers to seniors who've never used email. Oh, and you're spending $2M+ of the company's money while doing it.

What I Actually Did

Budget Allocation & Strategy:

  • How I allocated $2.24M: Built budget models in Excel comparing cost-per-enrollment across channels. Analyzed last year's performance, identified which products had best margins, which channels drove quality leads. Reallocated spend mid-season when digital was outperforming direct mail 2:1.
  • Balancing three product lines: Medicare Advantage got 60% of budget (highest enrollment volume), Supplement got 25% (higher margin, niche audience), Part D got 15% (defensive play to keep existing members). Adjusted monthly based on enrollment pace vs. goals.
  • Competitive response: Monitored competitor campaigns (what they mailed, when, what they offered). Adjusted our messaging and timing to differentiate—when competitors led with $0 premium, we emphasized network breadth and customer service ratings.

Campaign Execution:

  • Direct mail campaigns (primary driver): Managed 6-8 mail drops per enrollment period. This was our highest-performing channel by far—direct mail consistently drove 60%+ of total enrollments. Wrote creative briefs, reviewed designs, approved copy. Segmented lists by age, geography, current plan status. Tested personalization (variable data printing with member name, current plan, recommended plan). Tracked response rates by drop, segment, and creative version.
  • Digital advertising (supporting channel): Ran paid search (Google Ads targeting Medicare keywords), display retargeting, Facebook/Instagram (targeted 65+ with health/retirement interests). Built custom landing pages for each campaign with unique tracking parameters. Digital complemented direct mail but never matched its performance.
  • Agent enablement: Created sales tools for 50+ insurance agents. Built product comparison sheets, handled objections guides, enrollment checklists. Updated materials weekly during AEP when CMS changed rules or competitors launched new plans. Hosted weekly calls to train agents on positioning.
  • Email nurture: Built automated sequences in Salesforce Marketing Cloud. 7-touch series for prospects who requested info but didn't enroll. Personalized based on which product they looked at, which materials they downloaded. Included educational content (Medicare 101) early, then stronger enrollment CTAs closer to deadlines.

Compliance Navigation:

  • CMS approval process: Every marketing piece needs CMS approval. Submitted materials 60 days before use. CMS would come back with feedback ("can't say 'best,' need to add disclaimers, font too small"). Revised, resubmitted, waited another 2 weeks. Built timelines that accounted for this—started creative in March for October campaigns.
  • What you can't say: Can't use "free" (even for $0 premium plans), can't cherry-pick benefits without showing full benefit structure, can't use testimonials without specific disclaimers, can't target based on health conditions. Learned these rules inside and out so I could push creative boundaries without crossing compliance lines.
  • State regulations: Medicare rules vary by state. Kansas has additional requirements on font sizes, disclosure placement, contact information. Built templates that worked across all our markets while meeting strictest state requirements.

Results

Drove 150K+ member enrollments across all Medicare products. Successfully managed budget to hit enrollment targets while maintaining cost-per-enrollment efficiency. Built repeatable campaign framework that works across enrollment periods.

What I Learned

Marketing in heavily regulated industries requires a different skill set. You can't just "move fast and break things." You need to understand compliance, work within constraints, and still find ways to differentiate. This experience taught me how to operate in complex environments where the rules matter.

Neural Networks from Scratch

CS5720 NEURAL NETWORKS & DEEP LEARNING • FALL 2024

Why This Is Hard

Most people use TensorFlow or PyTorch. You import a model, call .fit(), and the framework handles everything. You never actually learn what's happening inside.

I implemented convolutional neural networks using only NumPy—no deep learning frameworks. That means I wrote the math for forward propagation, backpropagation, gradient descent, convolution operations, and pooling layers from equations. When training failed, I couldn't blame the framework. I had to debug the actual gradient flow through the network.

What I Built

  • LeNet-5 (1998 architecture): Classic CNN from Yann LeCun's original paper. Conv layer → pooling → conv layer → pooling → fully connected → output. Implemented exact architecture: 5x5 kernels, tanh activations, average pooling. Had to derive gradients for every layer by hand.
  • Mini-VGG (deeper network): Modernized architecture with 3x3 convolutions, ReLU activations, max pooling, batch normalization, dropout. More parameters, deeper network, harder to train. Required careful initialization and learning rate scheduling to converge.
  • CIFAR-10 Classification: 60,000 32x32 color images across 10 classes (airplane, automobile, bird, cat, deer, dog, frog, horse, ship, truck). This is a standard computer vision benchmark. State-of-the-art gets 95%+ accuracy with modern architectures and lots of tricks. My from-scratch implementation hit 70%+ just using the fundamentals.

The Technical Details That Took Forever

Implementing Convolution:

  • Convolution slides a small filter (e.g., 3x3) across the image to detect patterns. Each position computes a dot product. Sounds simple—implementing it efficiently in NumPy is not. Had to handle stride, padding, multiple input channels, multiple output channels, batch processing.
  • Forward pass: Nested loops would be too slow. Used NumPy's im2col trick to reshape input into a matrix, then matrix multiply. 100x faster.
  • Backward pass: Computing gradients with respect to inputs, weights, and biases. Each requires careful index management. One wrong dimension and the whole thing breaks.

Backpropagation Through The Entire Network:

  • Chain rule across every layer. Loss → FC layer → pooling → conv → pooling → conv → input. Each layer passes gradients backward to the previous layer.
  • Implemented gradient checking: compute gradients numerically and compare to my analytical gradients. If they don't match within 1e-7, something's wrong. Spent hours debugging grad check failures—usually a transpose or sign error.
  • Vanishing gradients were a problem initially. Activations were dying, gradients going to zero. Fixed with better initialization (Xavier/He init), ReLU instead of tanh, batch normalization, gradient clipping.

Optimization Algorithms:

  • Started with vanilla SGD. Too slow, unstable. Added momentum (accumulate velocity, smooth updates). Better but still slow.
  • Implemented Adam optimizer (adaptive learning rates per parameter). Combines momentum + RMSprop. More complex—need to track first and second moments for every parameter. But training became much more stable.
  • Learning rate scheduling: Start high (1e-3), decay over time. Implemented exponential decay and step decay. Experimented with warmup periods.

Results That Actually Matter

Hit 72% accuracy on CIFAR-10 test set after ~50 epochs of training. For reference: random guessing is 10%, simple linear classifier is ~40%, my from-scratch CNN is 72%, modern ResNets with tricks hit 95%+.

But the accuracy isn't the point. The point is I understand exactly what's happening at every step. I can explain why certain architectures work, why training fails, what each hyperparameter does, how gradients flow through the network. I didn't just use AI—I built it from the math up.

Why This Matters for Product Marketing

When an AI company tells me their model uses "deep convolutional architectures," I know exactly what that means technically. I know the engineering tradeoffs: deeper networks are more expressive but harder to train, larger kernels capture more context but increase parameters, pooling reduces compute but loses spatial information.

More importantly, I can translate this to customers. I can explain why their model is actually better (not just buzzwords), what the technical advantages enable, and what tradeoffs they made. Because I've made those same tradeoffs myself.

Sarcasm Detection System

COMPARATIVE ANALYSIS • NLP • FALL 2024

The Question

Everyone's using LLMs for everything now. But are they actually better than traditional ML for every task? I wanted to find out. So I built two sarcasm detection systems—one using classical neural networks (RNN/LSTM), one using LLM prompting—and compared them.

Approach 1: Traditional Neural Networks

  • Architecture: Embedding layer → LSTM → Dense layer → Binary classification
  • Training: Supervised learning on labeled sarcasm dataset
  • Inference: Fast, deterministic, runs locally
  • Cost: One-time training cost, then essentially free to run

Approach 2: LLM Prompting

  • Architecture: Prompt engineering with GPT-style models
  • Training: Zero-shot or few-shot learning
  • Inference: Requires API calls, slower, non-deterministic
  • Cost: Pay per token, ongoing operational cost

What I Found

  • Accuracy: LLM prompting had slight edge (~5% better) on edge cases and nuanced sarcasm
  • Speed: Traditional model was 10x faster at inference
  • Cost: Traditional model cheaper for high-volume applications
  • Explainability: Traditional model easier to debug and understand failures
  • Deployment: Traditional model simpler to deploy and maintain

The Answer

It depends. LLMs are better if you need maximum accuracy, have low volume, or want to iterate quickly. Traditional models are better if you need speed, have high volume, or want lower costs. There's no universal "best"—just tradeoffs.

Why This Matters for Product Marketing

AI companies love to say "powered by advanced AI" without specifying what that means. But customers care about the tradeoffs. This project taught me how to evaluate different AI approaches, understand the engineering decisions, and explain the actual value to customers. Not just "it's AI"—but "here's why this specific approach solves your problem better."
